1 Foundations

Introduction to Management Science

What it is, where it came from, and why it matters to a computer scientist.

1.1What is Management Science?

Management Science (MS) is the application of scientific and mathematical methods to solve business and organisational problems. It focuses on optimization, decision-making, predictive modelling, and simulation — turning messy real-world problems into models a computer or analyst can actually solve.

🗣️ Plain English Think of Management Science as "engineering for decisions." Just like a software engineer uses data structures and algorithms to make a program run efficiently, a management scientist uses mathematical models to make a business run efficiently — fewer wasted resources, faster decisions, better outcomes.

It's a discipline that combines mathematical modelling, statistics, and algorithms to support quantitative decision-making — instead of guessing, you build a model and calculate the best option.

1.2Evolution and Importance

Management Science didn't start in a boardroom — it started on a battlefield.

1940s — Origins in WWII Logistics
Born out of military need: resource allocation, convoy routing, and supply strategy. This is when linear programming was first developed to solve logistics problems under scarcity.
1960s–80s — Rise in Manufacturing & Operations
Moved into industry: inventory control, supply chains, and production scheduling. Operations Research (OR) grew rapidly as a business discipline.
2000s–Present — Integration with Data Science & Computing
AI, machine learning, and big data now enhance predictive analytics and real-time decisions. Cloud computing makes large-scale OR solutions possible for any company, not just governments.

This timeline is the whole story of the field in one line: military logistics → industrial optimization → data-driven decision-making. Today it's used across finance, logistics, operations, and IT.

1.3Intersection with Computer Science

Algorithms and optimization sit at the core of both fields. Management Science gives Computer Science the mathematical models; Computer Science gives Management Science the computing power to actually solve them at scale — in AI, cloud computing, and software engineering.

Synergy: combining analytical models with computing power is exactly what makes modern AI-driven decision systems possible.

1.5Role of Operations Research (OR)

Operations Research is the branch of Management Science that uses maths and statistics to optimize decisions. Its key techniques are Linear Programming, Queuing Theory, Simulation, and Game Theory.

2 Decision-Making

Decision-Making under Certainty and Uncertainty

Choosing the right tool depends entirely on how much you actually know about the future.

2.1Decision-Making under Certainty

Here, the outcome of every decision is known and predictable. You can use deterministic mathematical models — like payoff tables and optimization (e.g. Linear Programming) — because there's no guesswork involved.

💡 Real-Life Example Choosing the cheapest cloud provider when you already know all the pricing tiers, bandwidth costs, and your exact usage. There's no uncertainty — it's pure calculation.

2.2Decision-Making under Uncertainty

Here, outcomes depend on unknown future conditions (like market demand), so you must evaluate multiple possible scenarios. Tools include Decision Trees, Expected Value (EV), and the Maximin / Maximax / Minimax Regret criteria (covered in full in Module 5).

💡 Real-Life Example Investing in a new product without any market research data — you genuinely don't know if it will sell.

2.3Worked Example — Launching a New SaaS Product

Decision: launch the product or not. States of nature: High Demand (70% probability), Low Demand (30% probability).

DecisionState of NatureProbabilityPayoff
LaunchHigh Demand0.7$500,000
Low Demand0.3-$100,000
Don't Launch——$0
Expected Value (EV) = (0.7 × 500,000) + (0.3 × -100,000) = 350,000 - 30,000 = $320,000 Decision: LAUNCH (EV of launching > EV of not launching, which is $0)

2.4Takeaways

  • Certainty → deterministic models (e.g. Linear Programming)
  • Uncertainty → probabilistic tools (e.g. Expected Value, Decision Trees)
  • Combine data, logic, and intuition — a model is an input to a decision, not a replacement for judgement
  • Machine Learning is powerful, but it isn't always the right tool for the job — use the right model for the right problem
3 Optimization

Linear Programming and Optimization

The single most important tool in this course — used everywhere from cloud billing to CPU scheduling.

3.1What is Linear Programming?

Linear Programming (LP) is a mathematical technique used to achieve the best outcome (maximum profit, minimum cost, etc.) in a model whose objective and constraints are all linear equations or inequalities.

🗣️ Plain English You have a goal (maximise profit) and a bunch of limits (you only have so many hours, so much RAM, so much budget). LP is the systematic way of finding the exact combination of decisions that squeezes the most value out of those limits — instead of guessing and checking.

3.2Structure of an LP Problem

  • Decision Variables — the things you control (e.g. x and y)
  • Objective Function — what you want to maximise or minimise
  • Constraints — limitations or requirements (linear inequalities)
  • Non-negativity Constraints — decision variables must be ≥ 0 (you can't produce -5 units of anything)
Maximize profit = 5x + 4y Subject to: x + 2y ≤ 100 3x + y ≤ 90 x, y ≥ 0

3.3Worked Example — Software Company

Scenario: A company produces two software tools, A and B. A brings $40 profit and takes 2 hours of dev time; B brings $30 profit and takes 1 hour. Max dev time available is 100 hours, and at most 40 units of B can be sold.

Let x = units of A, y = units of B Objective: Maximize Z = 40x + 30y Subject to: 2x + y ≤ 100 (development time) y ≤ 40 (market limit on B) x, y ≥ 0 (non-negativity)

3.4Graphical Solution Method

For a 2-variable LP problem, you can literally draw it:

  1. Plot each constraint on a 2D graph as a line
  2. Shade the feasible region — the area satisfying all constraints at once
  3. The optimal point always sits on a corner (vertex) of that feasible region — test each corner in the objective function

Limitation: this method only works visually for two variables — beyond that you need the Simplex Method or software.

✅ Result Optimal solution: produce 30 units of A and 40 units of B, giving a maximum profit of $2,400. This shows the real value of the graphical method — you can literally see why that corner point wins.

3.5Worked Example — Cloud VM Allocation (Simplex)

Scenario: A cloud provider allocates two VM types. VM1 uses 4GB RAM and earns $10/hour; VM2 uses 2GB RAM and earns $6/hour. Total RAM available is 64GB, and at most 10 VM1s can be sold.

See the full formulation and Simplex solution
Decision Variables: x₁ = number of VM1 allocated x₂ = number of VM2 allocated Objective: Maximize Z = 10x₁ + 6x₂ Subject to: 4x₁ + 2x₂ ≤ 64 (RAM constraint) x₁ ≤ 10 (VM1 max limit) x₁, x₂ ≥ 0 Simplex Method Solution: x₁ (VM1) = 0 x₂ (VM2) = 32 Maximum Profit Z = $192/hour

Even though VM1 earns more per hour, it's more profitable to allocate only VM2s because VM2 uses half the RAM — you can fit far more of them into the 64GB budget. This is a great example of why intuition ("higher price = better") can mislead you, and why you actually run the numbers.

3.6Types of LP Problems

Product mix problems Diet / nutrition problems Transportation problems Assignment / resource allocation problems

3.7Guidelines for Formulating LP Models

  1. Define decision variables clearly
  2. Identify the objective function
  3. Extract constraints from the word problem
  4. Make sure all expressions are linear
  5. Include non-negativity conditions
⚠️ Common Mistakes Using non-linear expressions, forgetting non-negativity constraints, misinterpreting constraints from the word problem, and incorrectly defining variables. Most marks lost in LP questions come from formulation errors, not maths errors — read the scenario twice before writing anything.

4 Special LP Cases

Transportation and Assignment Models

Both are special forms of Linear Programming — one moves goods, the other matches tasks to workers.

4.1Transportation Model

Objective: minimise the total cost of distributing goods (or data, or requests) from several sources to several destinations.

  • Supply at each source
  • Demand at each destination
  • Cost of moving one unit between each source-destination pair
Minimize Z = Σ Σ Cᵢⱼ · Xᵢⱼ Cᵢⱼ = cost to transport from source i to destination j Xᵢⱼ = quantity shipped from i to j

Solution methods: Northwest Corner Rule, Least Cost Method, Vogel's Approximation Method (VAM).

💡 Real-Life Example Distributing computing workloads across 3 servers to serve 5 groups of users at the lowest possible latency — the "cost" here is network latency instead of shipping cost, but the maths is identical.

4.2Assignment Model

Objective: assign n agents to n tasks, one-to-one, to minimise total cost or maximise total efficiency. Each agent does exactly one task, and each task goes to exactly one agent.

Minimize Z = Σ Σ Cᵢⱼ · Xᵢⱼ Xᵢⱼ = 1 if task i is assigned to server j, else 0 (Balanced problem: number of agents = number of tasks)

Common algorithm: the Hungarian Method.

💡 Worked Example Assign 3 jobs to 3 servers to minimise total processing time. Optimal solution: J1 → S1 (GPU), J2 → S2 (CPU), J3 → S3 (Hybrid) — total time = 10 + 10 + 10 = 30 seconds.

Real-world tech examples

Data Centre Routing: Google's B4 software-defined network (SDN) uses similar assignment-style models for traffic engineering — matching data packets to network paths to minimise congestion and maximise throughput.

5 Strategy Under Uncertainty

Decision Analysis and Game Theory

What to do when you're not sure what will happen — and what to do when someone else is deciding too.

5.1Overview of Decision Analysis

Decision Analysis is a structured approach to making decisions under uncertainty or risk, using quantitative methods to evaluate options and choose the best course of action.

  • Decision Alternatives — the courses of action available
  • States of Nature — future events outside your control that affect the outcome
  • Payoffs — the outcome (profit, cost, utility) for each decision × state-of-nature pair
  • Decision Criteria — the rule you use to pick an alternative
EnvironmentCharacteristics
CertaintyOnly one state of nature; outcome is known
RiskProbabilities are assigned to each state of nature
UncertaintyNo reliable probabilities are available

5.2Decision Criteria Under Uncertainty

Try the example below — 3 possible IT investment strategies, 2 possible market states, and see how each decision rule picks a different "best" answer.

StrategyGood MarketBad Market
A — Aggressive expansion$120k-$60k
B — Moderate growth$70k$10k
C — Stay put$20k$20k
  • Optimistic (Maximax): assumes the best outcome will happen — pick the alternative with the highest possible payoff. Suits risk-takers.
  • Conservative (Maximin): assumes the worst-case scenario — pick the alternative whose worst outcome is the best among all worst outcomes. Suits risk-averse decision-makers.
  • Minimax Regret: build a "regret table" (how much you'd wish you'd chosen differently), then pick the alternative with the smallest maximum regret. Useful when the fear of missed opportunity matters.

5.3Tools Used in Decision Analysis

Decision Trees Payoff Tables Expected Value (EV) Sensitivity Analysis Utility Theory

5.4Decision Trees in Game Theory

A decision tree is a graphical tool for sequential decisions and outcomes. It has three types of nodes:

  • Decision Nodes (□) — a choice controlled by a player
  • Chance Nodes (○) — an outcome determined by probability
  • Terminal Nodes — final outcomes with a payoff attached

They're used to analyse sequential (extensive form) games, find Nash Equilibria in multi-step settings, and support backward induction — solving the tree from the end backwards. Common applications: bargaining games, entry-deterrence games, and investment decisions under uncertainty.

5.5Game Theory Basics

Game Theory studies decision-making in competitive environments, built around three ideas: Players (the decision-makers), Strategies (plans of action available to each player), and Payoffs (the rewards from chosen strategies).

DimensionQuestionTypes
CooperationCan players form binding agreements?Cooperative (yes) vs. Non-cooperative (no)
Payoff structureIs one player's gain another's loss?Zero-sum (yes) vs. Non-zero-sum (no)
TimingDo players act together or in turns?Simultaneous vs. Sequential
🗣️ Plain English Chess is zero-sum, non-cooperative, and sequential — my win is exactly your loss, we can't team up, and we take turns. Trade negotiations are usually non-zero-sum and can be cooperative — both countries can end up better off (or both worse off) together.

5.6The Prisoner's Dilemma

Two suspects are arrested and interrogated separately. Neither knows what the other will choose. Each can either stay silent or betray (confess). This is the single most famous example in Game Theory because it shows how two perfectly rational people can end up making each other worse off.

B Stays SilentB Betrays
A Stays SilentA: -1, B: -1A: -5, B: 0
A BetraysA: 0, B: -5A: -3, B: -3

Numbers = years in prison (negative; less negative is better). Highlighted cell = the actual outcome both rational players choose.

🔑 Key Insights Dominant strategy: both prisoners choose to betray, regardless of what the other does. Nash Equilibrium: mutual betrayal — neither player benefits from changing their choice alone. The paradox: mutual cooperation (both stay silent) would give a better combined outcome, but rational self-interest prevents either player from choosing it.
Real-world applications of the Prisoner's Dilemma
  • Business competition — price wars, where undercutting a competitor is individually rational but collectively destructive
  • International relations — arms races, where both sides would be better off disarming together but neither trusts the other to do it first
  • Environmental policy — overfishing and pollution, where individually rational resource use leads to collective harm

5.7Representation of Games

  • Normal Form: a payoff matrix showing strategies and outcomes (as above)
  • Extensive Form: a decision tree showing sequential moves
  • Characteristic Function Form: used for cooperative games

6 Predictive Tools

Forecasting Techniques

Using history to make an educated guess about tomorrow.

6.1What is Forecasting?

Forecasting means predicting future values based on historical data. It's vital for decisions in operations, inventory, and IT infrastructure planning.

  • Qualitative — expert judgement (used when there's little or no historical data)
  • Quantitative — time series and causal models (used when historical data exists)

6.2Time Series Analysis

A time series is a sequence of data points recorded over time (daily, weekly, monthly). It's usually made up of four components:

  • Trend — the long-term direction
  • Seasonality — recurring, predictable patterns
  • Cyclicality — longer-term economic cycles
  • Noise — pure randomness that can't be modelled away

Common tools: Pandas Series.plot() or an Excel line graph.

6.3Moving Averages

Moving averages smooth out noisy data by averaging over a rolling time window.

Simple Moving Average: SMAₜ = (1/n) × Σ Yₜ₋ᵢ Types: Simple Moving Average (SMA), Weighted Moving Average (WMA) Tools: Excel =AVERAGE(), Python rolling().mean()

6.4Exponential Smoothing

A weighted average that gives more weight to recent data than older data.

Sₜ = α·Yₜ + (1 - α)·Sₜ₋₁ α (alpha) = smoothing factor, between 0 and 1 Types: Single, Double, Holt-Winters Tools: Excel ToolPak, Python statsmodels

7 Intelligent Systems

Decision Support Systems (DSS) and AI/ML

Where Management Science meets modern AI — systems that help (or automatically make) decisions.

7.1What is a Decision Support System?

A DSS is an interactive, computer-based system that aids data-driven decisions. Its purpose is to solve semi-structured or unstructured business problems — the kind that don't have one obvious, purely mechanical answer.

🗣️ Plain English A DSS integrates Data + Models + a User Interface (plus AI, in modern systems) — think of it as the dashboard a manager uses to actually see and act on the results of all the models in this course, instead of running the maths by hand every time.

7.2Components of a DSS

Data Management

Example: sales records, customer data.

Algorithms, statistical models.
Example: linear programming for logistics.

User Interface

Dashboards, visualisation tools (e.g. Power BI).
Example: interactive reports.

Knowledge Engine

AI/ML models, expert systems.
Example: fraud detection rules.

Likely Exam Questions & Model Answers

Likely Question · Module 1 & 3

Q: Define Management Science and explain how Operations Research relates to it, using one CS example.

Management Science is the application of scientific methods to solve business and organisational problems, focused on optimization, decision-making, predictive modelling, and simulation. Operations Research is the specific branch of Management Science that uses mathematical and statistical techniques — like Linear Programming, Queuing Theory, and Game Theory — to actually solve those optimization problems. Example: using Linear Programming to optimise server resource allocation in a cloud data centre, balancing cost against performance.

Likely Question · Module 2

Q: Differentiate between decision-making under certainty and under uncertainty, with an IT example for each.

Under certainty, the outcome of each decision is known in advance, so deterministic models like Linear Programming apply — e.g. choosing the cheapest cloud provider once all prices are known. Under uncertainty, outcomes depend on unknown future conditions, so probabilistic tools like Expected Value and Decision Trees are used instead — e.g. deciding whether to launch a new SaaS product without knowing future demand.

Likely Question · Module 3

Q: Formulate an LP model for the following: A cloud provider allocates VM1 (4GB RAM, $10/hr) and VM2 (2GB RAM, $6/hr) with 64GB total RAM and a maximum of 10 VM1s sellable. State the objective and constraints.

Let x₁ = VM1 allocated, x₂ = VM2 allocated Maximize Z = 10x₁ + 6x₂ Subject to: 4x₁ + 2x₂ ≤ 64 x₁ ≤ 10 x₁, x₂ ≥ 0
Likely Question · Module 4

Q: What is the key difference between the Transportation Model and the Assignment Model? Give one CS application of each.

The Transportation Model distributes goods (or workloads) from multiple sources to multiple destinations to minimise total cost, and allows partial/multiple allocations. The Assignment Model is a special one-to-one case — each of n agents is matched to exactly one of n tasks — typically solved with the Hungarian Algorithm. Application: Transportation model for server load balancing across data centres; Assignment model for matching jobs to specific servers (e.g. J1→GPU server, J2→CPU server).

Likely Question · Module 5

Q: Using the Prisoner's Dilemma, explain the concept of Nash Equilibrium and why it can produce a worse outcome for both players.

A Nash Equilibrium is a state where no player can improve their outcome by changing their strategy alone, given what the other player is doing. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, both players betraying is the Nash Equilibrium — neither prisoner benefits from unilaterally switching to silence. However, if both had stayed silent, they would jointly receive a better outcome (1 year each vs. 3 years each) — showing that individually rational choices can lead to a collectively worse result.

Likely Question · Module 6

Q: Differentiate between Simple Moving Average and Exponential Smoothing as forecasting techniques.

A Simple Moving Average gives every observation in the chosen time window equal weight, which smooths out noise but reacts slowly to recent changes. Exponential Smoothing instead assigns more weight to the most recent observation (controlled by the smoothing factor α) and progressively less weight to older data, so it reacts faster to real recent shifts while still smoothing out random noise — this is why it's often preferred for tracking fast-changing metrics like server CPU load.

Likely Question · Module 7

Q: List and briefly describe the four core components of a Decision Support System.

Data Management — databases and data warehouses holding raw information (e.g. sales records). Model Management — the algorithms and statistical/optimization models applied to that data (e.g. linear programming for logistics). User Interface — dashboards and visualisation tools that let a human interact with the system (e.g. Power BI). Knowledge Engine — AI/ML models or expert-system rules that add intelligence (e.g. fraud detection rules).
